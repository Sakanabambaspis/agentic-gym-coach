# Level 5: Supplementation

## Core Idea
The least important level: most supplements "just burn a hole in your wallet," and even the best add only small effects. The chapter's real tools are **consumer-defense filters** — quality, validity, effectiveness — that make any supplement decision, in any year, evaluation-ready.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three-filter evaluation**:
  - **Quality** — does the bottle contain what the label claims? Industry reality: products regularly under-dose, omit actives, or contain unsafe/banned contaminants (an existential risk for drug-tested athletes). Quality signals: **third-party lab analysis** published (rare; some companies ship lab reports with orders), **USP / NSF / GMP certification stamps**, and **reputable raw suppliers** (e.g. Creapure creatine licensed into blends). Hard veto: **proprietary blends** — ingredients listed, doses hidden; the standard trick for under-dosing a long ingredient list.
  - **Validity & effectiveness** — does it do anything, is that thing relevant to muscle/strength, and is the effect big enough to matter? Research tools: **Examine.com** (Human Effect Matrix: outcome, direction, magnitude, % of studies agreeing, evidence grade) and **JISSN** (open-access position stands).
  - **New-supplement caution ladder**: no evidence → ignore; shaky surrogate endpoints → watch only; direct support from one group → wait for independent confirmation. Case study: **D-aspartic acid** — 2009 testosterone spike → industry pile-on → 2013 null on T/body comp/strength → later evidence of *decreased* testosterone in trained men. Even effective supplements are worth ~1–2%; missing the "next big thing" for a year costs nothing.
- **Protein spiking detection (applied quality check)**: some brands pad "protein" with cheap glycine/taurine to pass total-amino tests. Red flags: (1) price per lb of protein far below market (whey is a commodity — you'll never find it genuinely cheap); (2) proprietary blend or no leucine listed; (3) listed **leucine <2.7 g per 25 g protein** (wye's BCAA ≈25%, leucine ≈11%).
- **The recommended list (with doses)**:

| Supplement | Dose | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Multivitamin** | 1 normal-dosed multi/day | Cutting (micronutrient safety net); gaining — usually unneeded; avoid 4,000%-RDA "horse pill" bodybuilder multis |
| **Fish/algae oil (EPA+DHA)** | **2–3 g/day combined** | Cutting or gaining; leptin signaling, inflammation, mood, joint, disease-risk support |
| **Vitamin D3** | **9–36 IU/lb/day (20–80 IU/kg)** by sun exposure | Low sun/dark skin/indoor trainer; bloodwork is the ideal guide; skip in sunny months if outdoors often |
| **Creatine monohydrate** | **0.018 g/lb/day (0.04 g/kg)** — ~3–5 g for most | Any time; saturates in ~2 weeks then maintain. Timing/loading/carb-timing irrelevant long-term; monohydrate only (variants cost more or work less) |
| **Caffeine** | Tiredness: **0.45–1.40 mg/lb/day** (tolerance-proof) · Performance: **1.81–2.72 mg/lb (4–6 mg/kg)** ~30 min pre, **≤2×/week** | The only recommended pre-workout; tolerance builds fast — keep intake minimal so the big dose works when needed; start at half-dose |
| **Beta-alanine** | **3–4 g/day** | Only for continuous efforts ≥60 s (15+ rep blocks, long HIIT intervals); useless for strength/power training |
| **BCAAs** | **0.09 g/lb (0.2 g/kg) pre-workout** | Only if glycogen-depleted/low-carb/fast; redundant on adequate protein — evidence for trained lifters is scant |
| **HMB** | 3 g ~30 min pre-training | Cutting or extreme overreach, only with money to burn; not for gaining; trained-population evidence mixed |

## Key Concepts
- **Fish oil label math**: add EPA + DHA per serving, divide target by it (400 mg combined → 5 servings for 2 g).
- **Creatine mechanism reminder**: endogenous + from (raw) meat; cooking degrades it — hence supplementation for performance levels; benefits accrue to strength/power output and long-term muscle gain.
- **Beta-alanine placement logic**: buffering matters only in the 60 s+ anaerobic window; hypertrophy work doesn't require high reps (equal growth at 10RM vs 3RM equated volume), so the "bodybuilder default" is weaker than marketed.
- Multivitamin stance: harmless, possibly small long-term health benefit, a reasonable cut-phase insurance policy — never a substitute for Level 3 food variety.

## Anti-patterns
- **Proprietary blends**, "special sauce" formulas, dosing hidden behind trademarks.
- **New-release FOMO** — 99.9% of launched supplements disappear as useless.
- **Mega-dosed vitamin packs** — water-soluble vitamins at 4-digit %RDA are expensive urine.
- **BCAAs on a high-protein diet** — you already bought the BCAAs; they're in the whey and the meat.
- **HMB as a gaining-phase staple.**

## Worked Example
**The minimal evidence-based stack for a 180 lb lifter starting a cut**: creatine monohydrate (Creapure-sourced, plain) 3 g/day every day; fish oil reading "600 mg EPA+DHA per serving" → 4 servings ≈ 2.4 g; vitamin D3 at ~27 IU/lb ≈ 4,800 IU through winter, ~1,600 IU or none in summer; one normal multivitamin with food; caffeine only for the two heaviest sessions of the week (180 lb × ~2.3 mg ≈ 400 mg, half-dose to start). Total cost small, every item independently defensible — and none of it matters if Levels 1–3 are wrong.

## Key Takeaways
1. Filter first: quality (lab tests/certifications, no proprietary blends), then validity/effectiveness (Examine.com, JISSN).
2. Core five: multivitamin (cut), EPA/DHA 2–3 g, vitamin D3 by sun, creatine 0.018 g/lb daily, caffeine cycled and dosed by purpose.
3. Beta-alanine (60 s+ efforts), BCAAs (depleted/fasted cardio), HMB (money to burn while cutting) are situational at best.
4. New supplements: wait for independent replication — D-AA is the cautionary tale.
