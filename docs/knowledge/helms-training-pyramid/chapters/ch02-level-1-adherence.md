# Level 1: Adherence

## Core Idea
It doesn't matter how good your programming is if you cannot stick to it — a suboptimal plan you consistently execute beats an optimal plan you abandon. Design within the confines of what you can adhere to, using **REF: Realistic, Enjoyable, Flexible**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **REF (the three conditions for adherence)**: training must be:
  - **Realistic** — *When to use*: always, first. Match the program's **time frame** to your real deadlines (8 weeks to a meet ≠ an 8-week template crammed into 6 weeks) and its **schedule** to your actual week ("start with what you can do before you decide what you should do"). Optimal ≠ realistic; consistency makes progress, not perfection.
  - **Enjoyable** — *Why*: enjoyment drives effort, effort drives results, results drive more enjoyment (a positive cycle). Focusing only on the end goal while hating the process measurably *reduces* consistency (goal-focus vs process-focus study).
  - **Flexible** — *Why*: flexibility is what makes the other two possible when life intervenes. Implement via autoregulation: pick the easy, moderate, or hard session of your week based on how you feel — volume-matched flexible periodization produced greater strength gains than a fixed order in training studies.
- **Structured flexibility / autoregulation**: training stress should match your *total* ability to manage stress — life stressors (work, sleep, family) count against recovery whether or not you control them (people with more negative life events adapt less well to training).

## Key Concepts
- **"Optimal" is unknowable**: constantly questioning and changing your program in pursuit of the magic formula leaves you susceptible to marketing; "I should be progressing faster" is a trap mindset.
- **Interference effect**: concurrent endurance work doesn't erase or stop strength/hypertrophy gains — it *slows the rate* of gains. Mitigate by ordering: hard cardio *after* lifting < lifting ≥6h before cardio < separating them by a full day.
- **Priority principle for multi-sport**: one activity takes priority. Lifting in service of a sport = minimal volume/intensity, no failure, no soreness, no high-risk exercises. A competitive lifter who plays soccer recreationally doesn't run an "optimal" soccer conditioning program alongside.
- **Injury rates** (per 1000 hours): bodybuilding 0.24–1; powerlifting 1.0–5.8; weightlifting 2.4–3.3; CrossFit 3.1; strongman 4.5–6.1; Highland Games 7.5 — lower than basketball (8.5–11.1). Long-term risk isn't zero, but it's lower than mainstream sports and outweighed by health benefits.

## Mental Models
- Think of willpower as a **non-renewable resource**: "there is nothing impressive about choosing something that cannot be sustained."
- Think of life stress and training stress as **one cumulative stress bucket** — when the life side spikes, the training side must drop (structured flexibility).
- Think of "no plan survives contact with the enemy": derailment is inevitable; the *reaction* (stress, overreaction, program-hopping) is the real problem, not the disruption.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fitting a cookie-cutter program to the wrong time frame** (an 8-week template into a 6-week window).
- **Copying a pro's schedule** (6 days/week, 2h/day) while working 50h/week with family commitments.
- **Training through pain, or fleeing all training at the first niggle** — both extremes turn minor issues major. Middle ground: if it hurts, don't do it — alter range of motion, reduce load, or swap in a pain-free comparable movement (blood flow restriction can keep a growth stimulus at loads as low as ~20% 1RM for some single-joint work). See a specialist (PT/sports doctor with lifter experience) when it persists for weeks — not Instagram comments.
- **Cramming missed sessions together** in a high-intensity phase when simply shifting the plan back a few days is available.

## Worked Example
**The missed-session decision (as the authors walk it):**
1. **Off-season / no deadline**: do the missed session at the next outing. "I'll be behind!" — so what? Finish the plan a few days later than intended; it makes almost no difference long-term.
2. **Competition prep (deadline fixed)** — choose one:
   - **Skip it** if the missed session was low-stress by design: accessory day, grip/calves/arms day, or a power day of ~80% singles for technique. You keep the recovery benefit and lose only a small stimulus (or make it up on an unscheduled nearby day — unlikely to hurt the next workout).
   - **Rejig the week** if it was a high-priority session and skipping loses needed stimulus.
3. **Forced back-to-back hard days** (e.g. Mon–Thu–Fri): acceptable — three studies show consecutive-day vs split-day training yields similar strength and hypertrophy. Come in expecting <100%: **drop the load so the intended effort (RPE) stays where planned**, rather than rigidly hitting the numbers on the spreadsheet.

## Key Takeaways
1. "REF yourself" throughout program design: Realistic, Enjoyable, Flexible.
2. Design for the schedule you actually have; the plan you keep outranks the plan that looks best on paper.
3. Autoregulate by feel when it matters: swap in the easy session on a terrible day, not by abandoning the week.
4. Missed workout → pick up where you left off; near a competition → skip low-stress days or rejig.
5. Separate hard cardio and lifting (6h+ or a full day) to blunt the interference effect.
6. Pain is information: modify ROM/load/exercise first; escalate to a specialist only if it persists.
