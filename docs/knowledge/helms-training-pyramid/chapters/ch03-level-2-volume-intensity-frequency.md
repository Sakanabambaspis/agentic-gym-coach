# Level 2: Volume, Intensity, Frequency (VIF)

## Core Idea
Volume, intensity, and frequency are the foundation of a program — and they are **inseparable**: each changes what the others can be. High effort slows recovery (limiting frequency); heavy loads make each set costlier (limiting volume); cramming volume into few sessions degrades quality (frequency organizes the other two). They share one pyramid layer because you always set them *together*.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The VIF starting prescription** (novice/intermediate; average over a block, not every week):
  - **Volume**: 10–20 sets per muscle group / movement pattern per week (count overlap and heavy warm-up sets).
  - **Intensity (strength)**: ⅔–¾ of volume in 1–6 reps, remaining ¼–⅓ in 6–15 reps, all at 5–10 RPE.
  - **Intensity (hypertrophy)**: ⅔–¾ of volume in 6–12 reps, remaining ¼–⅓ split between 1–6 and 12–20 reps, at 5–10 RPE.
  - **Frequency**: each muscle group / movement pattern trained **2+ times per week**; usually 3–6 sessions per week total (twice/week can work for time-pressed novices).
- **Volume = hard sets, not volume-load**: count **sets in a given intensity zone**. Volume-load (sets×reps×load) and total reps distort: 3×25×100 yields 78% more "volume load" than 3×10×140, but 8–12RM sets produce equal hypertrophy per set to 25–35RM sets, and 3 sets of 2–4RM beats 3 sets of 8–12RM for strength despite less volume-load.
- **Strength vs hypertrophy specificity**: strength = muscle mass + neuromuscular adaptation + **skill** (specific to movement *and* rep range). Hypertrophy is comparatively non-specific — it grows from adequate volume at adequate effort across a wide rep range. Consequence: heavier training for strength, wider rep spread for size; even bodybuilders need some heavy work because being stronger makes volume easier to accumulate.
- **Fitness-Fatigue Model**: performance = **fitness − fatigue**. Both rise with training; fatigue masks fitness and dissipates faster. This is *why* dose-response is U-shaped, why deloads work, and why volume can't be added endlessly.
- **Functional overreaching → nonfunctional overreaching → overtraining**: planned overload that a deload converts into new PRs = functional. Pushing through declining performance to baseline or worse = nonfunctional ("wheel-spinning" — common). True overtraining = rare in lifting (seen mainly in contest-prep bodybuilders and high-intensity CrossFit competitors) and needs long recovery, not a deload.
- **Deload vs taper**: both reduce volume to let fatigue dissipate and reveal fitness. Taper = before a competition (peaking). Deload = inside a training phase. Deloads aren't just for recovery — they make the *next* block more effective.

## Key Concepts
- **%1RM → reps allowed (trained lifter averages; wide inter-individual spread — one study saw 9–26 reps at 70% 1RM)**: 100%→1, 95%→2, 90%→3–4, 85%→5–6, 80%→7–8, 75%→9–10, 70%→11–12.
- **RPE scale (reps in reserve)**: 10 = failure (no more reps or load); 9.5 = more load only; 9 = 1 RIR; 8.5 = 1 RIR, chance at 2; 8 = 2 RIR; 7.5 = 2 RIR, chance at 3; 7 = 3 RIR; 5–6 = 4–6 RIR. Experienced lifters estimate RIR accurately; beginners can calibrate (video review, short failure-exposure block, partner checks, AMRAP-to-failure checks).
- **Failure is a tool, not a default**: no strength advantage from training to failure (meta-analysis); failure groups showed overtraining markers and slower strength recovery. Never take big compounds (squat, deadlift, OHP) to muscular or technical failure — injury risk and systemic fatigue. Failure is cheap on isolation and machine work.
- **How light is too light**: >60% and <60% 1RM can produce equal hypertrophy at similar volume and high effort, but ~20% 1RM underperforms 40–80%. Practical floor: ~30% 1RM (~20–35RM) works but costs global fatigue ("junk volume" before the stimulus; RPE estimation degrades as reps per set rise; vomiting happened in the 25–35RM study arm). High-rep work suits isolation, machines, and painful joints — not heavy compounds.
- **Frequency is organization, not magic**: splitting equal weekly volume over more sessions improved hypertrophy (full-body 3× vs split, Schoenfeld) and strength/hypertrophy (Norwegian powerlifting team, 6× vs 3×); meta-analyses support 2+/week per muscle.
- **Movement overlap**: the body doesn't file exercises under one muscle. Squats + deadlifts share lumbar fatigue; a lat pulldown also trains triceps and chest; rows and OHP hit middle delts constantly (isolate middle delts only if advanced with a clear weak point).

## Anti-patterns
- **"More is always better"**: the moderate-volume groups won in both key studies (weightlifters: 2481 reps beat 1923 *and* 3030; biceps: 18 sets/week beat 9 and 27). Doing the *most* volume you can tolerate is not optimal; the minimum that still progresses is more sustainable but also not ideal — start in the range and adjust.
- **Adding volume prematurely**: do enough to progress; increase only when plateaued *and* recovering well. Heavier loads at the same sets/reps already prove overload.
- **Prescribing bare %1RM without RPE**: "3×8×70%" buries some lifters at RPE 9–10 while others cruise at 3–4.
- **All-heavy-all-the-time for size**: the 3RM group grew the same as the 10RM group but took ~4× longer per session, had more joint pain and dropouts. All-light: puke-fare compounds and unreliable failure detection.
- **Daily 1RM / extreme specificity as a default**: the Bulgarian method works (case series) but sacrifices volume per session, multiplies fatigue, and is at best a short intensification/overreach tool — not a career-long template.

## Worked Example
**Combining %1RM with RPE** — prescribe 3×8 @ 70% @ 6–8 RPE:
- Lifter with poor rep-endurance hits RPE 9 on set 1 → **drop load** next set to land in 6–8 RPE.
- Lifter with great rep-endurance floats through at RPE 4 → **add load**.
Only set 1 is off-target, and each lifter self-corrects — the RPE ceiling/floor makes a single prescription work across very different people.

**Reading the plateau flowchart**: Plateaued? → First check the free wins: sleeping 8+ h? energy surplus? protein ≥0.7 g/lb (1.6 g/kg)? honest RPE? each muscle 2×/week? technique solid? Fix any "no", run another mesocycle. → Then: recovering? (Dreading the gym? worse sleep quality? load/reps *decreasing*? worse life stress? worse aches? Yes to 2+ = not recovering) → Not recovering → temporarily reduce volume (light week); if you fatigue again immediately, permanently run less volume or reorganize it. Recovering → it may be time to *increase* volume.

## Key Takeaways
1. 10–20 hard sets per muscle/movement per week, 2+×/week frequency, mostly 1–15 reps at 5–10 RPE — novices start low (~10–13 sets), intermediates/advanced mid-to-high (~14–20).
2. Count sets in intensity zones; ignore volume-load and raw reps.
3. Strength needs specificity (heavy, skill practice); hypertrophy needs volume at effort — 6–12 is convenient, not magic.
4. RPE/RIR is the adjustment dial that makes load prescriptions fit individual anatomy.
5. Failure only with a purpose: isolation, last sets, testing — not big compounds, not every set.
6. Volume grows over a *career*, not every month; increase only when plateaued and recovering, deload when fatigue wins.
