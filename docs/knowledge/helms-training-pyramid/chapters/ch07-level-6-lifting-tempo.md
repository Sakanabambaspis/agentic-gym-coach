# Level 6: Lifting Tempo

## Core Idea
Tempo is the least important level — the goal is simply *not getting it wrong*: **a controlled (not slow) eccentric, and a forceful concentric with maximal intent to accelerate**. Deliberately slow lifting trades load and volume (the big-ticket items) for time under tension — and tension has a magnitude as well as a duration.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Impulse, not just time under tension**: hypertrophy stimulus from a physics view = **force × time** (impulse, the area under the force curve). TUT alone is incoherent — you're under tension all day resisting gravity and you don't grow. The load matters: volume-equated squats at 3×12 @ 70% vs 6×12 @ 35% (all reps at maximal intent) gave the light group more time under tension, velocity, and power — yet the heavy group produced **~25% more impulse**.
- **Why slow eccentrics defeat their own purpose**: you're ~20–40% stronger eccentrically than concentrically (less energy cost, no inertia to overcome, passive structural loading). Eccentric training works in labs because it lets you *lift heavier* than concentric strength allows. In the gym, your load is capped by concentric strength — the chain's weakest link — so a 4-second lowering phase just adds fatigue while *reducing* the load you can handle. You get the downside of eccentrics without the mechanism.
- **The evidence on slow tempo**: meta-analysis — rep duration has **no effect on hypertrophy until reps reach ~10+ seconds, where it becomes negative**. Deliberately slow training is inferior in most studies (lower loads, equal-or-worse outcomes). Maximal-intent concentric velocity produced almost **double the bench strength gains** vs deliberately half-speed concentrics.
- **For strength, the load sets the tempo**: control the eccentric to arrive in position, then push with maximal intent (the bar won't move fast at 95%, but intent is the point — that's what makes a lifter good at grinding). Powerlifting nuances: a controlled descent positions the squat; a slower, settled bench touch earns the press command faster.
- **Supramaximal eccentric training** (2nd ed. addition): heavier-than-concentric-max lowers via spotter ("3 more!") or machines (two limbs up, one limb down). Logically attractive — real overload beyond the concentric ceiling — but the evidence isn't there yet: no significant advantage for hypertrophy (15-study meta) or strength (5-study meta). Add the logistics: hard to implement and track, dangerous with free weights, extra soreness and acute performance suppression. Verdict: an experimental block for advanced lifters in expert-supervised settings only.
- **The legitimate exception**: deliberately slow heavy lifting (2–4 s phases @ 60–85% 1RM) helps tendon pain/rehab — a clinical tool, not a growth tool (and not the authors' scope to prescribe).

## Key Concepts
- **Tempo notation**: 2/0/2 = 2 s eccentric / no pause / 2 s concentric — roughly how most people already lift moderate loads.
- **4/0/2 vs 2/0/2 findings**: 4-second eccentric → lower 1RM, ~1.5 fewer reps on a 75% AMRAP, and ~**10% less total work** — "ironically, by focusing on increasing total time under tension, you can decrease total volume and load."
- **The beginner caveat**: slowing down *is* appropriate for novices — as a form-learning tool, not a growth mechanism.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counting "Mississippi's" on every eccentric** to grow — you'll sacrifice load, reps, and total impulse.
- **Letting gravity catch the weight** — a controlled eccentric means *you* own the lowering; gravity-dropping isn't an eccentric muscle action.
- **Grinding slow max attempts as a novice** — the ability to grind is a trained skill, not a starting point.
- **Adopting supramaximal eccentrics on current evidence** outside supervised experimentation.

## Worked Example
**The impulse thought experiment (ankle-weight marathon):** if duration of tension were what mattered, walking a marathon in ankle and wrist weights (6 hours of tension!) would beat an hour of hard lifting. Nobody believes that — which is exactly why "slower = more growth" fails. When a set slows down so much that load or reps drop, total impulse usually drops with it. Test any tempo idea against the impulse frame: did force × time go up, or did I just make the force smaller for longer?

## Key Takeaways
1. Just lift the weights — controlled eccentric, maximal-intent concentric, good form.
2. Tension has magnitude *and* duration; protect impulse by protecting load and reps.
3. Never slow a lift so much that the load or rep target has to fall.
4. Slow tempo belongs in rehab (clinician-directed) and novice form practice — not hypertrophy programming.
